Ungulate Health
Concerns with ungulate health are rising with the recent detection of Adenovirus Hemorrhagic Disease (AHD) in B.C. and detection of Chronic Wasting
Disease (CWD) within the southeast part of the Kootenay Region. AHD was first identified in the fall of 2020 on several Gulf Islands (Galiano, Mayne,
Pender, and Salt Spring) and southern Vancouver Island as far north as Merville. AHD is a highly contagious and often fatal disease, particularly in
black-tailed deer. It has caused significant losses of deer in the U.S.
The disease course is usually rapid as the virus damages small blood vessels in the lungs and intestines. Symptoms of AHD include difficulty
breathing, foaming, or drooling from the mouth, diarrhea, and seizures. If an infected animal survives the acute phase, chronic signs may include ulcers
and abscesses in the mouth and throat that can lead to weight loss and death. Fawns/juveniles are more susceptible to AHD and experience much
higher death rates than adults. Affected animals are often found dead and in good body condition with no signs of trauma or other cause of death.
AHD can be spread through direct contact between deer or contact with fluids (saliva, feces, urine). Transmission may also occur through airborne
routes, contaminated feed and water, and contaminated equipment. There is no evidence that AHD can be transmitted to humans and research
supports that it is not transmitted to livestock or pets. Although there is no known risk to human health from AHD, hunters are advised not to consume
meat from animals found dead, obviously ill or acting abnormally prior to death. To help prevent the spread of AHD, dispose of carcasses by deep
burial, do not move infected, dead or live deer to new areas, and prevent aggregations of deer at artificial feeding or water sources.
CWD has been detected in B.C. and the risk of further spread continues to increase as high-risk material is moved around the province or brought into
the B.C. Natural movement of infected animals puts certain parts of B.C. at greatest risk, but the disease could be introduced anywhere, including
Vancouver Island, with the transport of infected carcasses and use of CWD contaminated material (i.e., urine-based scents). Help protect B.C.
ungulates by understanding these risks, helping to increase awareness, and submitting your deer, elk, moose or caribou for testing. Learn more about
the BC CWD Program at www.gov.bc.ca/chronicwastingdisease and AHD at www.gov.bc.ca/wildlifehealth.

The Kootenay Region offers a diversity of wildlife and habitats and significant opportunities to enjoy the outdoors. Every person should have a stewardship mindset
to take care of the impact they may have on the living and non-living components. Biologists have a few important reminders for hunters in the Kootenay Region:
• Chronic Wasting Disease was confirmed in white-tailed deer and mule deer populations in the Kootenay Region in January 2024.
• An emergency response has been initiated to prevent spread and access samples to better understand the extent of the outbreak.
• A high risk boundary (the CWD Management Zone) that includes MUs 4-1 to 4-8 and 4-20 to 4-25 has been defined based on animal movement and proximity to
CWD cases in BC, Alberta and Montana.
• Mandatory testing is expanded to include deer, elk and moose harvested within the CWD Management Zone and samples must be submitted to a designated
freezer before leaving this area. For freezer locations see www.gov.bc.ca/CWDdropoff
• There is a 24-hour exemption after harvest on specified routes for deer, elk or moose harvested in M.U. 4-7, M.U. 4-8, and M.U. 4-25 that require travel outside
of the CWD Management Zone to immediately access a designated CWD freezer. See page 15 for specific routes.
• Brain tissue and spinal column (except for the tail) from deer, elk and moose harvested within the CWD Management Zone cannot be transport outside of this
boundary. It is recommended to leave these parts at or near the kill site or dispose of them at a landfill before leaving the area.
• We encourage hunters to continue hunting in the affected areas; hunting can help to suppress transmission and spread by removing infected animals from the
landscape. It is estimated that less than 1% of cervids currently have CWD in these areas.
• Hunting is the most important tool for managing CWD as it helps to suppress disease prevalence and provides essential information to support science-based
management of CWD.
• For more information visit: www.gov.bc.ca/chronicwastingdisease

Wildlife Management Update from the Omineca Region

Results from population monitoring since 2020 suggest that moose numbers have stabilized in the southern and central parts of the region
and have slightly increased west of Prince George. While the recent trends are positive, numbers are still well below densities observed in the
early 2000s. Research on the factors influencing moose population dynamics continues (www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/environment/plantsanimals-ecosystems/wildlife/wildlife-conservation/moose/moose-conservation/moose-research) and moose habitat work has provided
moose-friendly approaches to forest harvesting, including Ungulate Winter Ranges (UWRs) for moose where forest harvesting is paired with
management requirements to maintain or improve moose habitat. Inventory work continues for elk, mule deer, thinhorn sheep, mountain
goat, caribou, grizzly bear, wolf, lynx, fisher, and marten. Collaborations among Ministry biologists, First Nations, and stakeholders are further
investigating limiting factors and monitoring approaches for mountain goats, elk, and thinhorn sheep in the region. Harvesting a collared
animal is not illegal if taken lawfully. If you harvest a collared animal, please report it to the RAPP line to confirm whether the animal is safe
to eat due to the possible presence of wildlife immobilization drugs in the meat and return the collar to the Conservation Officer Service or
any Ministry office. BC strives to maintain the health of wildlife populations in collaboration with our partners for the benefit of all British
Columbians. Our goals are to ensure that Indigenous hunters can exercise their right to hunt within their Traditional Territories and to promote
licensed hunter and trapper opportunities, including resident experience and a sustainable guide-outfitter industry.
